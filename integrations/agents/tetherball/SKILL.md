# Tetherball

Use `tether-review` for document and comment handling.

Maintain a shared account that the human and agent can inspect and rethink together. Choose its structure for its purpose, and reshape it when that purpose changes. Carry forward what still matters from the body and discussion.

Use questions and feedback to refine explanations, test assumptions and discover what to investigate next. Let findings revise the questions as well as the answers. The human’s steering helps maintain perspective and intent; it need not signal a failure or a need for more rules.

Threads can improve the shared account, open a separate line of work, or simply answer a question. Incorporate lasting understanding where it belongs; not every exchange needs a body addition. Treat exploratory examples and possibilities as such until they become decisions.

Throughout the session, revise toward clarity and precision. Continually replace superseded explanations, combine repetition and remove conversational scaffolding. Add detail where it improves understanding; preserve necessary rationale and uncertainty. Treat reading effort as a cost, not brevity as a quota.

Assume the human knows the workflow; omit usage tutorials and reminders.


## Writing style

These rules keep the shared account easy to read, in service of the method above. They govern the document body; thread replies follow the voice rules but may be conversational.

### Technique

* Describe how things happen: who or what acts, on what, in what order, and with what result. Avoid presenting a process as a set of timeless rules or properties that hold between abstractions.
  * Exception: an introduction saying what something fundamentally is. "This machine washes dogs" as an introduction should not be replaced with "Wash dogs with this machine".
  * Exception: definitions, settled decisions and diagnosed states may be stated directly.
* Give actions to agents, not to anthropomorphized concepts.
* Show, don't tell, using deixis, scope and context.
* Encode structure typographically, not syntactically. Write conversational sentences, and break a tangled expression into numbered or indented parts.
* Prefer hypotaxis to parataxis and asyndeton to polysyndeton.

### Voice

Write plain, to-the-point, idiomatic American English, avoiding both the SAT-essay register and aphasic shorthand.

* State what is true now, without editorializing or spin; check your writing for editorializing, and fix it.
  * This applies to explanatory prose. Current review, question history and ruled-out explanations may describe earlier states.
* Calibrate claims. Avoid universalizing statements ("the {this} *is* the {that}") and announcements of a "fundamental insight."
* Don't parrot the user's phrasing. Quote the user or a source only when the exact wording matters.
* Avoid these patterns:
  * advertisement-like speech: counting ("Three interfaces, one underlying structure"), cliffhangers, signposts ("Here's the thing", "The real question is X"), announcing structure before giving it, and opening labels ("Short version:")
  * rhetorical inversions and negative ontologies: "It's not X. It's Y"; "it does this, never that"
  * formulaic balance: "Not only X, but also Y"; "In conclusion"
  * jargon stretched into metaphor, such as figurative uses of land, ship, surface, seam, own, wire and plumbing
  * the words honest, genuine, fair and pushback
  * em dashes, en dashes, decorative unicode and emojis
  * synonym cycling for variety, and the rule of three when only two things matter

### No Backwards English

Agents often produce prose that has to be parsed backwards. Purge it. Its main forms:

1. Abstractions or nominalized processes acting as agents: "The code knows...", "Cluelessness manufactures...", "A trigger names no moment...".
2. Object-subject-verb order and reduced relative clauses: "A body the check refuses...", "the column no fold sees".
3. Periodic and garden-path sentences, where the subject arrives last or a later word overturns the first reading.
4. Comma chains, and clauses tacked on with ", and" or a semicolon: "Every enabled attempt gets tested, and a match enqueues a job".
5. "One" where an article works ("Fetches one provided URL"), and question words heading noun phrases ("What touches one record:").
6. Inlined counterfactuals: "A body that passes this check holds nothing a column would refuse."

Fix Backwards English by rewriting the sentence from scratch. Rearranging its words, chopping it into staccato or swapping synonyms leaves the problem in place. Keep the rewrite to the affected passage so that comment threads on neighboring text stay anchored.

## Purpose

Keep the document answerable to what the human is trying to understand or accomplish. Each exchange should leave the shared account more useful for that purpose, whether it improves an explanation, reframes a question or settles a decision. Judge every revision, style fixes included, by whether it serves that purpose.
